## Part I

### Normal Mapping
Normal mapping is a performant technique that allows us to add fine detail to objects without extra geometery. It works by using a normal map texture to pertube the normals across an objects surface, this is passed on through the rest of the lighting calculation to display as if there was actual changes in the geometery.

The original lighting system operated in the world space, however normal maps describe the surface in tangent space. Therefore, to implement normal mapping we first needed to construct the TBN (tangent, bitangent, normal) matrix to transform the normals from tangent space to world space. To do this we utilise the fact that the tangents are already provided for us and can be extracted from the models in `ModelLoader` like we already do for normals. We then followed the way normal data was passed through to all the way to the shaders and did the same for the tangents, modifying the `EntityRenderer` and `BaseLitEntityShader` files accordingly.

Because we modified the Entity Renderers to include a normal map slot, we also needed to create a default normal map texture. For this we copied the implementation of the `default_white_texture` and intialising it to `rbg(128, 128, 255)` in `TextureLoader.cpp`. We then made sure to include this everywhere that the Entity Renderers were called.

Finally we began work on the actual shader implementation for regular and animated entities. For the vertex shader, we used the Gram-Schmidt process to construct the TBN matrix from just the normal and tangent vector, making sure to do it after bone transformation in the animated entity shader. This is then passed to the fragment shader where we sample the normal map, then multiply it by the TBN matrix to transform the perturbed normals into world space. 

Passing the modified `ws_normal` vector into the original lighting calculation results in the lighting effect coming out as intended.

The main issues we ran into with this was a roadblock at the start with getting the tangents, however we figured it out once we started looking at the ModelLoader code. We also spent some time struggling with the compilation before we realised we had not updated the calls to Entity Renderer not including the new default normal map. Finally we spent some time debugging a weird lighting issue on the normal maps, which turned out to be the result of importing them in sRGB format which was easily fixed.

For this feature we were primarily guided by the tutorial on https://learnopengl.com/Advanced-Lighting/Normal-Mapping. This really helped me get my head around the TBN transformations. The part at the bottom about working with complex objects is what got me started modifying the `ModelLoader`.

### Post Processing
This part of the editor was something we had wanted to do from the start to make our demo scene come to life. We didn't want anything too complicated, so we settled for three effects in our stack: vignette, film grain and some simple colour grading tools.

To implement our post-processing, we had to modify the way the program rendered the final image. We now render the entire scene to our own frame buffer object (using the GL_RGBA16F flag to get 16 bit colours for HDR), and then draw that frame to a full screen quad.

On this quad, we run our post-processing fragment shader for each pixel, to calculate our effects.

**Vignette:**
A very simple vignette just makes pixels darker based on their distance to the corner. Our vertex shader calculates our screenspace coordinates `texCoords`, and we just get the distance to (0, 0) and calculate a gradient based on that.

**Colour Grading:**
We got our colour grading formulae from https://learnopengl.com/Advanced-Lighting/HDR

Our Exposure simply works by mapping our HDR brightnesses back down to [0, 1] using the formula.
Our Contrast works by reducing each brightness by 0.5, then multiplying by the contrast, before adding 0.5 back again. 
Our Saturation works by calculating the luminance of the image (with some funny numbers), then interpolating between the original image and the greyscale image based on the saturation level.

**Film Grain**
Our film grain works by using a noise function to generate a random number for each pixel, then multiplying it by the film grain strength and adding it to the colour. 

### Sky Box
Our skybox also required adding a new Renderer and shader. We modified the vertex shader to pass through a world space position and then use the fragment shader to sample the texture. 

We changed the way that the cubemap was rendered by modifying the cubemap shader to remove the projection matrix and instead just pass through the world space position. We then take the dot product of this and the view matrix to get the final texture coordinates, effectively removing the projection matrix.

The cubemap renders on its own layer, and then the whole scene is rendered on top. We made sure to use the depth-fail method in the fragment shader to prevent the cubemap from being drawn behind objects in our scene.
