Normal mapping is a performant technique that allows us to add fine detail to objects without extra geometery. It works by using a normal map texture to perturb the normals across an objects surface, this is passed on through the rest of the lighting calculation to display as if there was actual changes in the geometery.

The original lighting system operated in the world space, however normal maps describe the surface in tangent space. Therefore, to implement normal mapping we first needed to construct the TBN (tangent, bitangent, normal) matrix to transform the normals from tangent space to world space. To do this we utilise the fact that the tangents are already provided for us and can be extracted from the models in `ModelLoader` like we already do for normals. We then followed the way normal data was passed through to all the way to the shaders and did the same for the tangents, modifying the `EntityRenderer` and `BaseLitEntityShader` files accordingly.

Because we modified the Entity Renderers to include a normal map slot, we also needed to create a default normal map texture. For this we copied the implementation of the `default_white_texture` and intialising it to `rgb(128, 128, 255)` in `TextureLoader.cpp`. We then made sure to include this everywhere that the Entity Renderers were called.

Finally we began work on the actual shader implementation for regular and animated entities. For the vertex shader, we used the Gram-Schmidt process to construct the TBN matrix from just the normal and tangent vector, making sure to do it after bone transformation in the animated entity shader. This is then passed to the fragment shader where we sample the normal map, then multiply it by the TBN matrix to transform the perturbed normals into world space. 

Passing the modified `ws_normal` vector into the original lighting calculation results in the lighting effect coming out as intended.

The main issues we ran into with this was a roadblock at the start with getting the tangents, however we figured it out once we started looking at the ModelLoader code. We also spent some time getting the texture buffers and pathways set up correctly, following the layout of the other textures. We then spent some time struggling with the compilation before we realised we had not updated the calls to Entity Renderer not including the new default normal map. Finally we spent some time debugging a weird lighting issue on the normal maps, which turned out to be the result of importing them in sRGB format which was easily fixed.

For this feature we were strongly guided by the tutorial on https://learnopengl.com/Advanced-Lighting/Normal-Mapping. This really helped us get our head around the TBN transformations. The part at the bottom about working with complex objects is what got us started modifying the `ModelLoader`.

This part of the editor was something we had wanted to do from the start to make our demo scene come to life. We didn't want anything too complicated, so we settled for three effects in our stack: vignette, film grain and some simple colour grading tools.

To implement our post-processing, we had to modify the way the program rendered the final image. We now render the entire scene to our own frame buffer object (using the GL_RGBA16F flag to get 16 bit colours for HDR), and then draw that frame to a full screen quad.

On this quad, we run our post-processing fragment shader for each pixel, to calculate our effects.

**Vignette:**
A very simple vignette just makes pixels darker based on their distance to the corner. Our vertex shader calculates our screenspace coordinates `texCoords`, and we just get the distance to (0, 0) and calculate a gradient based on that.

**Colour Grading:**
We got our colour grading formulae from https://learnopengl.com/Advanced-Lighting/HDR

Our Exposure simply works by mapping our HDR brightnesses back down to [0, 1] using the formula.
Our Contrast works by reducing each brightness by 0.5, then multiplying by the contrast, before adding 0.5 back again. 
Our Saturation works by calculating the luminance of the image (with some funny numbers), then interpolating between the original image and the greyscale image based on the saturation level.

**Film Grain**
Our film grain works by using a noise function to generate a random number for each pixel, then multiplying it by the film grain strength and adding it to the colour.

We then changed the way that the cubemap was rendered by modifying the cubemap shader to remove the projection matrix and instead just pass through the world space position. We then take the dot product of this and the view matrix to get the final texture coordinates, effectively removing the projection matrix. The skybox renderer renders last (we set this in `MasterRenderer.cpp`), but we made sure to use the depth-fail method in the fragment shader to prevent the cubemap from being drawn behind objects in our scene. This essentially fills in all the empty pixels left from the other renderers.

Parallax mapping builds on normal mapping to create the illusion of surface depth without additional geometry. While normal mapping perturbs surface normals to fake lighting detail, parallax mapping displaces texture coordinates based on the view direction and a depth map, producing a convincing 3D depth effect that can obscure other parts of the surface unlike normal mapping. 

Since the TBN matrix and tangent pipeline were already in place from the normal mapping implementation, the main work was adding a depth map slot to the rendering pipeline. We added the `depth_map_texture` parameter to `BaseLitEntityRenderData`, bound it to texture unit 3 in `EntityRenderer` and `AnimatedEntityRenderer`, and added a `depth` uniform to `BaseLitEntityMaterial` (defaulting to `0.0f`). Entities without a depth map fall back to the existing `default_black_texture`, which gives a depth value of zero and effectively disables the effect. On the editor side, we added a "Depth Map" texture selector and a "Depth" slider (0.0–0.1) to both `EntityElement.cpp` and `AnimatedEntityElement.cpp`. We also needed to add this to `SceneElement.cpp` to get saving and loading to include the new parameter.

For the shader, we implemented steep parallax mapping following the LearnOpenGL tutorial. The fragment shader transforms the view direction to tangent space via `transpose(TBN)`, then the `ParallaxMapping` function steps through depth layers to find the displaced texture coordinate. The total offset is `P = viewDir.xy * depth`. The layer count adapts dynamically from `minLayers = 8.0` to `maxLayers = 80.0` based on the viewing angle via `dot(vec3(0,0,1), viewDir)`, giving higher quality at grazing angles while staying cheap when looking straight on. A while-loop steps through layers until the sampled depth exceeds the current layer depth, at which point the surface intersection has been found. The resulting `parallax_uv` replaces `scaled_uv` for all texture lookups, and fragments outside `[0, uv_scale]` are discarded to prevent edge artifacts.

Following the same guide, we later enhanced it to use parallax occlusion mapping, which adds interpolation between each depth layer to smooth out the effect and significantly hide the stepping artifacts. This is done by averaging out the two closest layers to the intersection point, giving a much smoother result. 

Initially we had planned to stick with the most basic implementation, but the initial results were very dissapointing and easily broke down. This led to us looking deeper into steep parallax mapping from the tutorial, which wasn't much more work to implement once we had the basic function, so we upgraded our shaders to utilise it. Another concern about this feature is the heavy impact it has on performance, whilst the view angle optimisation helps a lot, at grazing angles the performance is more than halved.
When building the scene we also realised the initial approach of offsetting the outwards created issues on the edges of 3d models, this was easily fixed by inverting the offset to move inwards instead when reading the depth map.

For this feature we were guided by https://learnopengl.com/Advanced-Lighting/Parallax-Mapping, particularly the sections on steep parallax mapping and dynamic layer count adjustment.
